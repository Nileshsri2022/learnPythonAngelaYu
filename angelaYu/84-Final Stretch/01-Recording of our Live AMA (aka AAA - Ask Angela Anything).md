Here is a structured breakdown of this live AMA (Ask Angela Anything) session.

---

### 1. On Languages: They're Tools, Not School Subjects

* Angela still mixes up syntax when switching between Swift, JavaScript and Python — good
  linters and editors catch the mechanical mistakes so she can spend attention on logic.
* "Do I need to master one language before another?" — depends on the goal. Building an iOS
  app? Get serious about Swift. Occasionally need a website? Learn just enough of it.
* **A language is a hammer, not a subject.** You don't need 100 % hammer proficiency before
  picking up a saw.

---

### 2. Competition and Getting Hired

Asked whether remote work makes jobs more competitive:

* Anything desirable is competitive — focus on your own output instead of the crowd.
* **"So good they can't ignore you"**: become excellent at the thing you do and the
  opportunities find you.
* To get a job as a full-stack developer, **think like the hiring manager**. They receive
  thousands of applications and can't read them all, so they look for evidence of
  capability.
* **Show, don't tell:** build and deploy real web apps. Tutorial clones don't stand out.

---

### 3. How to Read Documentation

* Quality varies wildly — Flutter's docs are exemplary (example-first, parameters
  explained); Apple's are famously terse.
* Don't read docs like a dictionary. Learn what you need, when you need it: search,
  look at an example (often on Stack Overflow), then read the docs for the details.
* Information is cheap now; the skill is finding and applying it, not memorising it.

---

### 4. Learning a Skill (the German example)

Angela's method for learning German — a template for any skill:

1. Break the goal into components (speaking, listening, vocabulary).
2. Find a way to practise each component repeatedly.
3. Use tooling to get feedback (Google Translate listening to her spoken German; Netflix's
   *audio description* track to train listening by matching language to visible action).

Apply the same to coding: break the project down, practise the parts you're weakest at, get
feedback fast.

---

### 5. Startups and Freelancing

* **There is no perfect time to start.** She built her company during night shifts as a
  junior doctor, working on the second and third days off after each block.
* Iterate like she did at 13: try delivering Yellow Pages directories, decide manual labour
  isn't the strength, print tutoring flyers instead, iterate again.
* For freelancers starting out: **charge far below what you think you're worth at first.**
  Build reputation, reviews and referrals, then raise prices as demand grows. You're buying
  experience, communication skills and testimonials.
* Pricing software: price by appeal — mass-market products can be cheap; niche products
  must cost more to cover the effort.

---

### 6. UI/UX

* Don't treat UI/UX as the last step — "sprinkling UX magic dust" at the end doesn't work.
  Design decisions belong at the start of a project (Day 65's pillars).
* Study great experiences: Monzo's card activation (tap the card with your phone, done)
  versus a traditional bank's branch visits and phone calls.

---

### 7. Miscellaneous

* No language is "more powerful" — hammer vs saw again.
* Web development vs software development: a matter of taste; both are viable career paths.
* Remote work and internships existed even before the pandemic, and remote positions remain
  available.

---

### Summary Checklist

1. Languages are tools; get fluent in what your goal requires.
2. Competition is normal — become undeniable, and show your work in a portfolio.
3. Learn documentation on demand, via examples first.
4. Break any skill into parts and drill them with fast feedback.
5. Start before you feel ready; iterate; undercharge at first to earn reputation.
6. Design early, not at the end.
